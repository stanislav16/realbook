The Lamp at Carrick Head
by RealBook Sample

Chapter 1: The Keeper

The lighthouse at Carrick Head had been kept by the same man for forty-one years, and Angus MacLeod intended to keep it for forty-one more. He was an old man now, broad and stooped, with a white beard that the wind combed sideways and a deep, gravelly Scottish voice that seemed to come up from the rocks themselves. He spoke slowly, as if every word had to climb the stairs first.

Isla Quinn was nine years old, and she had decided that the lighthouse was the most important building in the world. She was small and freckled, with a high, quick voice and a habit of asking three questions before anyone could answer the first.

She found him on the gallery that morning, polishing the great glass lens.

“Is it true you’ve never missed a night?” Isla asked. “Not even one? Not even when you were sick?”

“Not one,” said Angus. “The ships don’t care if I’ve a cold.”

“What if there’s a storm?”

“Then they need the light more, lass, not less.”

Isla leaned over the rail and looked down at the water heaving against the rocks. “Mum says there’s a storm coming tonight. A big one. She heard it on the wireless.”

The old man grunted. “Your mother hears a great many things on that wireless.”

Ellen Quinn ran the post office in the village below, and she had come to Carrick Head from Galway twelve years before with a suitcase, a baby, and an Irish accent that she had never quite lost. She was a sensible woman in her thirties, and she worried about her daughter climbing a hundred and twelve steps every morning to bother an old man.

She was waiting at the bottom of the tower when Isla came down.

“You’re to leave Mr. MacLeod in peace,” Mrs. Quinn said firmly. “He has work to do.”

“He likes me,” said Isla.

“He tolerates you, love. That’s a different thing entirely.”

Angus appeared in the doorway behind them, wiping his hands on a rag. “She’s no bother, Mrs. Quinn. She asks good questions.”

“She asks too many,” Mrs. Quinn replied, but she was smiling.

Chapter 2: Visitors

The visitors arrived on the afternoon bus, just as the sky turned the colour of a bruise.

The first was a tall young American in a rain jacket far too thin for the weather. His name was Theo Barnes, and he wrote for a magazine in New York. He talked fast, the way people do when they are used to being interrupted, and he had a camera around his neck and a notebook in every pocket.

The second was Dr. Priya Raman, a young marine scientist from Mumbai, who had come to count seabirds on the cliffs. She was quiet and precise, with a warm, measured voice, and she carried a pair of binoculars as if they were a part of her.

“So this is the famous lighthouse,” Theo said, staring up at the tower. “Forty-one years, one keeper, never a night missed. Do you know how good that story is? Do you know how many people would read that story?”

“I’ve no idea,” said Angus.

“Thousands. Maybe a million. Can I get a photo of you by the door? Maybe looking out to sea, very serious, very weathered?”

“I’m always weathered,” the old man growled. “It’s the weather that does it.”

Priya lowered her binoculars. “The birds are flying inland,” she said quietly. “All of them. The gannets, the kittiwakes. They only do that when something very large is coming.”

Isla tugged at her sleeve. “Is it the storm? Mum said there’d be a storm. Is it going to be a bad one? Will the birds be all right?”

“The birds know what they’re doing,” Priya said gently. “It’s the boats I would worry about.”

Angus looked out at the darkening water for a long moment.

“Then we’d best light the lamp early,” he said. “Isla, run and tell your mother to bring candles and the big kettle. Mr. Barnes, you can carry oil. Doctor, you’ll want to see this from the top.”

“Seriously?” said Theo. “You’re putting me to work?”

“You wanted a story,” said Angus. “Now you’re in one.”

Chapter 3: The Long Night

The storm came in at nine o’clock, and by ten the whole tower was humming like a struck bell.

Rain hammered the glass. The lamp turned and turned, throwing its long white arm across the sea, and in the lamp room the five of them crowded together with mugs of tea that Mrs. Quinn had made on the little stove.

“I’ve covered hurricanes in Florida,” Theo said, his voice rising over the wind. “I’ve covered a volcano in Iceland. I have never been this scared in my life. Is the tower supposed to move like that?”

“It’s supposed to stand,” said Angus. “And it will.”

Mrs. Quinn put an arm around her daughter. “Are you frightened, love?”

“No,” said Isla. Then, after a moment: “A bit.”

“A bit is the right amount,” the old man said. “A bit keeps you careful.”

Priya was at the window with her binoculars, though there was nothing to see but rain. Suddenly she stiffened.

“There,” she said sharply. “Two points off the headland. A light. A small one, going up and down.”

Angus was beside her in three strides, faster than any of them had seen him move. He looked for a long time.

“A fishing boat,” he said at last. “The Margaret, by the look of her. Hal Dunn’s boat. He’s too close to the Teeth.”

“The teeth?” Theo asked.

“The rocks below the point,” said Mrs. Quinn quietly. “They’ve taken more boats than anyone can count.”

Angus turned to the lamp. “He can see our light,” he said, “but he can’t see the rocks. So we’ll show him.” He reached for the old signal lamp that hung on its hook by the door, the one with the red glass. “Isla. You know the Morse I taught you?”

Isla nodded so hard her hair flew. “Yes. Yes, I know all of it. What do I send?”

“Send ‘rocks, turn north.’ Over and over. Don’t stop until I tell you.”

So Isla Quinn, nine years old, stood at the window of the lighthouse at Carrick Head in the worst storm in twenty years and flashed a red light into the dark, while an old man held her steady, an American wrote nothing at all in his notebook, a scientist counted the seconds aloud, and her mother whispered prayers in a Galway accent that had come all the way back.

The little light on the water hesitated. Then, slowly, it turned north.

“He’s seen it,” Priya said. “He’s turning. He’s clear.”

Theo let out a breath he seemed to have been holding for an hour. “That,” he said, “is the story.”

“No,” said Angus, looking down at the girl with the signal lamp still clutched in both hands. “She is.”
